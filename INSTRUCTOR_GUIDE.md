# Instructor Guide · Online Dating Pairing Game v0.2

## Learning goal (25–40 minutes)

Illustrate how reciprocal choices and rejection can produce matching between partners with similar *assigned* desirability scores, even when students do not know their own fictional scores. Discuss limits of applying a simple classroom simulation to actual online dating.

## Setup (5 minutes)

1. Open the deployed application and choose **Create classroom**.
2. Choose 4–40 fictional roles. For smaller classes, use a role count close to actual participants; unused roles remain unavailable until students join.
3. Display only the room code and ask students to open the application on their own device and join. Each joining device gets a fictional role.
4. Remind students they are playing fictional roles. They should *not* reveal their real-life preferences, personal information, or romantic history.
5. Students who would rather not role-play should have an observer/analyst option; no individual participation should be required.

## Play (10–15 minutes)

1. Start the game once students have joined.
2. Each participant can see the fictional score of other joined roles; their *own* score stays hidden.
3. They can send **one pending matching request at a time**. A recipient can accept or decline. A successful pair exits the available pool.
4. When useful, click **Next round** to clear unresolved requests and give students a fresh opportunity to choose.
5. Encourage students to make decisions as if the numbers are all they know. They should not communicate their actual assigned scores.

## Reveal and reflection (10–15 minutes)

1. Select **Reveal scores & results**. Students will see their own fictional score and the pairing outcomes.
2. Teacher results include a score scatterplot, pairs table, unmatched count, and CSV export. The graph describes role assignments, not student attractiveness.
3. Ask:
   - Did students pursue high-scoring profiles? What happened after a rejection?
   - Did final partners have similar assigned scores? Why might this occur—or not occur?
   - How could increased access to potential partners on dating apps alter this process?
   - What does this game leave out about real attraction, identity, and relational compatibility?
4. Exit ticket: "In 100–150 words, interpret the simulated results using the matching hypothesis and identify a limitation of the exercise."

## Theoretical caution

The instructor should **not** teach a match as inevitable, or claim the game proves dating apps cause assortative mating. The score is a deliberately simplified social-value proxy. Explain that matching may emerge from mutual choice and rejection; actual partner choice is multidimensional and context-dependent.

## Classroom adaptations

- **Online synchronous:** Share the HTTPS game link and code, then facilitate the reveal over video conference.
- **In-person:** Display the teacher dashboard on a projector; students join on personal devices, or opt for observer roles.
- **Large enrollment:** Use separate classroom rooms led by teaching assistants or run several sessions rather than one oversized room.
- **Accessibility:** Avoid mandatory smartphone use; students can use laptops, or study the instructor demonstration without playing. Respect students who choose not to role-play.

## Technical limitations

Sessions exist only in server memory, with a maximum lifetime of 6 hours. A server restart or free-host sleep loses the session. The published free-host version is a teaching pilot, not an archival data collection service. Results can be exported as anonymous CSV before ending the session.
